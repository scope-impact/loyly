JUXTCast, Chris Ford, What Coding Agents Still Need From Us

The Ford overlap

Two accounts of working with coding agents, written without knowledge of each other. One comes from consulting at scale, on other people's legacy systems. One comes from a single operator, and exists in four implementations of the same method at different levels of completeness, only one of which is public. The interesting part is not that they agree. It is which things each side had to build that the other never needed, and how much of ours a reader can actually go and look at. The right-hand column is a record of what has been built, not a status board: one of its items ran for a while and was retired on cost, and the marks say so.

Four implementations, and which of them you can inspect

The right-hand column is the method as built, not the method as described, and not the method as running this week: an item is listed once it has been built and run somewhere, and one of them is no longer run anywhere. Every item carries the same four-slot mark. The slots are always in the same order and a slot is either its letter (present) or a dot (absent), so ..KP reads as the third and fourth only. A lowercase letter is the one further state: ...p means the fourth implementation built it and has stopped running it. Position and letter-case carry the meaning, not colour, which is what makes the marks survive a theme switch and a photocopier.

L
Löyly, the public demonstration, deliberately minimal: one slice, one domain, the whole machinery small enough to read in an evening. This is the one you can go and look at (github.com/skallinen/loyly), and it is the reason the marks exist, because seven of the thirteen are in it and six are not.
S
Saunavuoro, the same shape against a real corpus of transcribed cooperative mail, with a working harness directory and five method pages written against it. A private company repository.
K
Kalibra, a calibration trainer whose core is arithmetic, which is what bought it the oracle and the first reality audit anyone here has actually run. Private.
P
The production pipeline, the fullest version: it regenerates an entire application from its specification every week, unsupervised, and scores the result. It is not ours to show. Nothing of it appears on this page except the mechanisms, which is the same register its own distillation uses.

The marks cut both ways, and the sibling grid page counts the cost. The cell Ford calls informative feedback, giving the agent eyes, was filled in two implementations of the four: a reality audit driving the real interface has been run in the trainer and in the production pipeline, and never in either sauna repository. Every finding on record in those two was found by reading a document. Neither of those two runs it today, which is why the last item in the right-hand column carries a lowercase mark: our position in that cell is a loss rather than an absence.

His only

Everything here follows from having colleagues, clients and a codebase older than the method. We have none of those.

Harnesses shared by a team
An organisation packages a harness so others can make new checks with it, rather than each person rebuilding one. [38:13]
Socialised, not just shared
Shipping the artefact is not enough: it has to be accompanied by explaining to people why they should care. A single operator never notices this cost. [38:41]
Custody as four quadrants
Is the code durable, is the spec durable, and has the team agreed which one it is looking after. He gives the vocabulary for the position we occupy and had never named in one sentence. [29:50]
Guardrails have a shelf life
A normative harness written for today's model may be dead weight against a better one. Nothing in any of our four implementations has an expiry date on it, and it should. [43:48]
Two days becomes an afternoon
The barrier was never knowing what would help, it was the two days you could not justify to a sprint. This reframes our whole instrument from rigour, which sounds like discipline nobody has time for, into something to try on Monday. [37:18]

Both

Six mechanisms two independent attempts both arrived at. Convergence is the evidence here, not agreement.

Decision records
A record exists to keep the branch that was not taken, so a later reader can see what was pruned and why. Our decision logs and his answer the same need.
Guides as context
A map of the domain handed to the agent before it writes anything. His guides, our wiki and contexts. Same cell of his own grid.
Elicitation by interaction
He objects to making an expert review generated markdown, on the grounds that it is not what you would do for a new colleague. Our workshop ran recorded rounds with the room and merged the transcripts, without having heard the objection.
Marking its own homework
A check the agent can iterate against, inside the loop. He means it approvingly. We say done is ratified, never self-asserted, which is the same mechanism with the opposite rhetoric.
Tools the model writes
Have the model build a deterministic tool, then run the tool without the model. The checklist generator, the id stamper and the scoreboard are exactly this, and all three are in every implementation.
Plural, multimodal specs
Prose, scenarios, statecharts and a hand-worked fixture, so different people can describe what they want in the form that suits them. We had four modalities and had never said so.

Ours only

Everything here answers a question he does not have to ask: how does one person, who both writes the spec and grades it, avoid grading themselves. Seven of the thirteen are in every implementation. Six are not, and the marks say which, including one that was built and is no longer run.

In every implementation

The destructive cut LSKP
An observation not written into the spec by the deadline dies with the implementation, on purpose, because the code cannot carry it forward. Nothing in the episode destroys anything deliberately. It runs weekly only in the production pipeline; in the other three it is demonstrated rather than lived.
A fence, proven on the graph LSKP
The clean room is physical: the prior implementation and every measurement are deleted from the working tree and from the object graph, and the probe checks the graph rather than the tree, because a weaker probe once passed while last week's answer was still readable one commit back. It asserts the property, not the procedure believed to imply it.
Ids that outlive the wording LSKP
Every scenario carries an id stamped once and never derived from its own words, so an editorial pass over the spec cannot silently reset a week-over-week comparison.
Honest denominators LSKP
Zero verdicts is a grading run that did not happen, not a score of zero, and an item the grader never reached is filed as a failure, so a gap can never quietly shrink the denominator. A moved denominator is restated out loud. Both rules were bought: a scoring run whose every batch failed on an outage once committed scored over an empty record.
A seeded holdback LSKP
A quarter of the checklist is scored and never listed, membership computed from a seeded hash at report time, and the gap between the visible rate and the held-back one is the alarm for fitting the spec to the checklist. This is the mechanism with no counterpart anywhere in the episode. It is also weaker than it sounds: not materialised is not the same as not computable, and the seed and the hash both survive the fence, so inside the clean room the held-back set can be enumerated in one command. That hole is written up rather than fixed.
An instrument, not a harness LSKP
His definition of a feedback harness is one that reports back into generation. Our most valuable measurement never reaches the agent at all: it reports to the operator, once a cycle. By his definition it is not a harness, and keeping the two words apart is worth doing. The machinery is installed in all four, and only the production pipeline has ever produced a scored cycle from it.
Docs that name no stack LSKP
No language, framework or database appears anywhere in the specification, ever, and the harness's entire contract with an implementation is two executables: run the suite, serve the app. A small rule, and it is what buys the last item in this column.

Only in the fuller ones

A probe only the app can pass ··K·
A gate once ratified a build that was serving the framework's starter template: three suites green, the port answering, the page correctly titled. A name is carried by templates, error pages and placeholders; the vocabulary a product is specified in is not. The probe now reads the compiled artefact and requires the product's own domain words, and it was proved against a stub that had the name and none of them.
An independent oracle ··KP
A third implementation of the core arithmetic, written from the prose rather than from any running system, because two tracks agreeing proves only that they agree. It carries a precedence rule fixed in advance, so nobody decides under pressure which of the spec and the oracle is the bug. The two sauna repositories have nothing of the kind, and one of them says so in writing.
Unattended rounds, on a clock ···P
The loop runs itself: rounds against a deadline, the gate run from outside after each one rather than by the agent reporting its own result, a backoff when a limit is real, and a ledger and a live stream so an unattended night is still observable. Each cycle ends deployed, with a record of what was decided. The other three are driven by a person deciding when to stop.
Contracts compiled from docs ···P
A generator walks the specification and emits the machine-readable contracts, and the build regenerates them, so the document and the contract cannot disagree: one is computed from the other. The demonstrations hold the same rule by discipline alone, which is a weaker thing.
A perception pass, since retired ···p
An agent was handed a plain-language user goal, phrased the way a user would phrase it and naming no selector, endpoint or internal function, drove the real interface through a browser toolbelt, and recorded a journey log and screenshots. That record was then assessed by a separate judging pass, not by the agent that produced it. It is the cell Ford calls informative feedback, built and staffed, with the independence the cell's value depends on. It was retired on measured cost rather than on principle: judging cost several times what generating cost, against a weekly capacity limit that cannot be bought through. So this is the one row where the mark says was, and it is the more interesting claim, because a loss is harder to say than an absence and it qualifies the economics argument in his own column: this improvement was affordable to build and unaffordable to run every week.
One spec, three stacks ···P
The same specification built three times with the instrument held still, so the stack is the only variable. Portability stops being a claim and becomes a number. It is the item a reader is most likely to assume the public demonstration can show, and it cannot: one implementation has ever run it, and it is the one nobody outside can see.

One axis and a half, not a third axis

The tempting claim is that we added an axis to his grid: he classifies a harness by what it says, and half our mechanisms are about custody, who may see the harness and who may write it. That claim is half right, and the honest version is stronger for being smaller.

Visibility is derived, not additional. A previous run's output, or the scoring criteria, is feedback about one round that becomes feed-forward input to the next. Fencing it is not a new dimension, it is the crossing noticing that a signal moved quadrant. Independence is genuinely additional. Neither axis can ask who authored the verdict, and that is a question about provenance rather than about the timing or the bindingness of a signal. A gate the claimant does not control differs from one they wrote in no way either axis can express.

So: one axis and a half. It is a smaller claim than the one we wanted to make, and unlike the larger one it survives contact with the grid. It also explains why the column above is the length it is. In a team, independence arrives for free with other people: somebody else wrote the check, somebody else reads the report, somebody else does not know which cases you skipped. One operator working with agents gets none of that, and an agent is not a second person, so the independence has to be constructed piece by piece. That construction is most of what the fuller implementations have and the smaller ones do not.

Where the two actively conflict

These are not regions of a Venn. Each is a place where his recommendation and our practice point in opposite directions, and a diagram that folded them into his only would be hiding the interesting part.

SubjectHis positionOurs
Spec-lock The agent should be able to update the specification as it learns. Edits to the spec during a build round are reverted, in all four. Specs change in planning rounds, on purpose.
Rebuild from spec Sceptical of Phoenix architecture, regenerating a system from its description. In the production pipeline it is the main loop, weekly and unsupervised. In the other three it is a demonstrated step rather than a lived cadence, which is a distinction the argument has to keep. And our own instrument gives him partial support, which is worth saying out loud.
Greenfield Demonstrations on new code mislead people about what the method costs on legacy systems. Three of the four are greenfield. The fourth is a real system with real users, but it was built under this method from its first commit, so it is not the legacy case either. The objection lands and we have no answer from evidence.
Markdown Piling up generated markdown scales worse than it looks, and asking an expert to read it is not respectful of their time. The method is markdown most of the way down.
Quality is optional Some software does not warrant this care, and saying so is honest. Cuts against the implicit pitch of any method presentation, including ours.
